"Beautiful ring, perfect for any occasion." Every jeweler has written it, and it tells a buyer nothing. It tells Google and ChatGPT even less. When someone asks an AI tool for "a 14k gold oval solitaire under $3,000 in Los Angeles," it looks for pages that say exactly that. Your product description is where the piece gets its facts, its story and its chance to be recommended. Here's how to write one that does all three.

Why Descriptions Matter More Now

A buyer in your store can hold the ring, ask about the setting and feel the weight. Online, the description does that job. AI search raised the stakes: tools like ChatGPT, Google AI Overviews and Perplexity pull details straight from product pages when they answer shopping questions. A page that clearly states metal, stone, size and price gives them something to quote. A page that says "stunning" gives them nothing.

Start With the Facts

Put the details a buyer would ask about first, in plain words, near the top.

  • Metal: karat and color, like "14k yellow gold" or "platinum 950."
  • Stone: type, shape, carat weight and, for diamonds, the grades you can stand behind. Say "lab-grown" when it is.
  • Size and fit: ring size options, chain length, band width, stone measurements in millimeters.
  • Price or price range: pages with prices answer the question buyers type most.
  • Made where and by whom: "Made to order in our Los Angeles studio" is a fact, and a strong one.
  • Care, sizing and returns: one line each, or a link.

Pro tip: write the facts as a short spec list under the first paragraph. Shoppers skim it, and search engines read it cleanly.

Then Add the Part Only You Can Write

Facts get you found. The story gets you chosen. Two or three sentences are enough: why you designed it, what it looks like on a hand in daylight, who usually buys it. Write it the way you'd say it across the counter.

If your description could sit on any other jeweler's site, it isn't finished.

Pro tip: keep a note of what clients say when they try a piece on. "It looks bigger than it is" or "I can wear it to the gym" is better copy than anything you'll invent at a desk.

A Before and After

Before: "A stunning ring that is perfect for any occasion. Elegant and timeless."

After: "A 1.2 ct oval lab-grown diamond set low in 14k yellow gold, so it sits close to the finger and won't catch on sweaters. Our most requested engagement ring for clients who want something they can wear every day. Made to order in our Los Angeles studio in about three weeks. Sizes 3 to 9."

The second version answers five questions before anyone asks them, and every line is something an AI tool can repeat accurately.

The Rules You Can't Bend

The FTC's Jewelry Guides cover how metals and gemstones may be described. In short: say "lab-grown" or "laboratory-created" for created stones, don't call a plated piece "gold," and only use karat marks that match the metal. Diamond grades should match the grading report. Overstating a piece online creates the same problem as overstating it in person, and it's in writing.

Help Search Read It

  • One page per piece with its own title, like "Oval Lab-Grown Diamond Solitaire, 14k Yellow Gold."
  • Product structured data with name, image, price, availability and reviews where you have real ones. Google documents exactly which fields it reads.
  • Descriptive image file names and alt text that match the description.
  • No copy-paste from suppliers. If ten stores carry the same text, nothing marks yours as the source.

Pro tip: ask ChatGPT "what does [your store] sell in engagement rings?" If the answer is vague, your product pages are too.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a jewelry product description include?

Metal and karat, stone type and size, measurements, price, where it's made, care and sizing details, plus two or three sentences on what makes the piece special.

How long should a jewelry description be?

Usually 80 to 200 words: a short opening, a spec list and a few lines of story. Signature and bridal pieces can run longer.

Do I have to say a diamond is lab-grown?

Yes. The FTC's Jewelry Guides say created stones must be clearly described as lab-grown, laboratory-created or similar.

Can I use AI to write jewelry descriptions?

Yes, as a first draft. Check every metal, stone and grade against your records and add details only you know, like how the piece wears.

Do product descriptions help AI search?

Yes. AI tools quote clear, specific product details when they answer shopping questions, so a factual, unique description makes your piece easier to recommend.
