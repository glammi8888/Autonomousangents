What Is Hyperpigmentation?

Hyperpigmentation is the term for patches of skin that are darker than the skin around them. It happens when pigment cells called melanocytes make extra melanin in certain areas. It is very common and usually harmless, but it can be frustrating because it often fades slowly.

What Causes It

The most common triggers are sun exposure, inflammation from breakouts or irritation and hormonal changes. Sun exposure also darkens existing spots, which is why protection is part of every plan.

Pro tip: any new spot that changes in size, shape or color should be checked by a board-certified dermatologist before any cosmetic treatment.

Common Types of Dark Spots

Most dark spots fall into three groups, and each behaves a little differently. Knowing which type you have helps you choose the right approach.

Sun Spots

Sun spots, sometimes called age spots, are flat brown spots that appear on areas that get the most sun, such as the face, hands and chest.

Marks Left After Breakouts

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is the flat dark mark that stays after a pimple, rash or irritation heals. A review in PMC notes it is especially common in skin of color.

Melasma

Melasma appears as larger patches, often on the cheeks, forehead or upper lip, and is linked to sun exposure and hormones. It tends to return, so it usually needs ongoing care rather than a one-time fix.

Which Layer of the Skin Do Dark Spots Sit In?

Dark spots can sit in different layers of the skin, and that changes the plan. Melanin is made by melanocytes in the bottom layer of the epidermis, the outer layer of the skin. Pigment that stays in the epidermis usually responds better to topical treatment, while pigment that has moved deeper into the dermis responds poorly to topical products alone, according to a published review.

Hyperpigmentation Treatment Options

Hyperpigmentation treatment usually combines daily sun protection, targeted skincare and, when needed, in-office treatments. Published reviews describe sunscreen as the foundation, with other options added on top.

Daily Sun Protection

Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen helps prevent new spots and keeps existing ones from darkening. One review recommends products with physical blockers such as zinc oxide or titanium dioxide, and another found that adding daily sunscreen improved results for melasma.

Topical Treatments

Common topical options include hydroquinone, retinoids and azelaic acid. Hydroquinone works by blocking an enzyme the skin needs to make melanin, and results often appear after about 5 to 7 weeks. Strong products can irritate the skin or cause side effects with long-term use, so they should be used under a provider's guidance.

Pro tip: introduce one new active product at a time. Irritation can itself cause new dark spots, which works against your goal.

In-Office Treatments

When topical products are not enough, providers may suggest in-office options such as chemical peels or laser and light treatments. Why Fall Is a Good Time to Start

Many pigmentation treatments make the skin more sensitive to sun for a while, and sun exposure darkens spots. Starting in fall, when days are shorter and you spend less time outdoors, gives your skin a calmer season to respond. In Los Angeles the sun stays strong year-round, so daily sunscreen still matters.

Pro tip: take a photo of your skin in the same light every month. Pigment fades slowly, and photos make progress easier to see.
