Why Your Board Stopped Working

Saving feels like progress. You see the linen apartment, the studio with north light, the woman reading in a café at 10am on a Tuesday, and you tap save. For a second it feels like you did something. Then the feed moves on and so do you.

There's research behind that feeling. Psychologists Heather Kappes and Gabriele Oettingen found that indulging in positive fantasies about an ideal future can lower the energy people put into getting there. A board you only add to can turn into exactly that: a place where the wanting gets used up.

Keep pinning. Just treat the board as raw material, not the finished thing.

Step 1: Edit It Down

Open the board and pick the 15 pins that still make your chest tighten a little. Move them to a new secret board. Everything else stays where it is. You don't have to delete anything; you just stop pretending all 400 are the plan.

Tip: if two pins say the same thing, keep the one you'd be embarrassed to show anyone. That's usually the honest one.

Step 2: Say What Each Pin Means

An image of a kitchen isn't a goal. "I want to cook dinner for friends in a place I chose" is. Under each of your 15 pins, write one line in the description: what is it you actually want here? A feeling, a place, a version of your week.

This is where it gets uncomfortable, and where it starts to work. Vague wants are easy to keep. Specific ones ask something of you.

You've saved enough. Do something with it.

Step 3: Give Every Image One Move

For each pin, write the smallest real step you could take this week, with a day and a time. Not "get fit." "Thursday 7am, the reformer class two blocks away, one time." Not "move to Lisbon." "Sunday night, look up what a one-month rental actually costs."

There's evidence for this. Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran reviewed 94 studies on these "when and where" plans and found they made people meaningfully more likely to follow through. A study by Lien Pham and Shelley Taylor found the same thing from another angle: picturing the process beat picturing the outcome.

Tip: if a move feels too big to do this week, it isn't the first move yet. Make it smaller until it's almost silly.

Step 4: Put It Where You'll Actually Look

A board you have to remember to open is a board you won't open. Choose one: a phone wallpaper made from your top three pins, a printed page on the fridge or a weekly five-minute check on Sunday night where you look at the 15 and ask what you did.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you make a vision board on Pinterest?

Create a board, save images that match the life you want, then edit it down to about 15 pins, write what each one means and add one small action with a day and a time.

Should my Pinterest vision board be secret?

It's up to you. A secret board can make it easier to be honest about what you want, since only you and anyone you invite can see it.

How many pins should a vision board have?

Fewer than you think. About 10 to 20 is enough to stay focused; hundreds of pins make it easy to look at everything and act on nothing.

Do Pinterest vision boards work?

A board alone won't change anything. Research suggests clear goals and specific plans for when and where you'll act are what make follow-through more likely.
