The law of assumption is the idea that your life follows what you assume is already true about yourself, so the way to change it is to live from the assumption that you already have what you want. It comes from the teacher Neville Goddard, and the useful part is simple: when you act like the person who already has it, you start making that person's choices. The part where people get stuck is assuming, then waiting.

Where the Law of Assumption Comes From

Neville Goddard taught it from the 1940s onward, in books like Feeling Is the Secret. His line was "assume the feeling of the wish fulfilled": picture the scene that would happen after you got what you want, feel it as real and keep living from that place. TikTok gave it a second life, mostly as stories about texts from exes and dream jobs appearing out of nowhere.

So let's be honest about it. There's no evidence that assuming something makes it appear on its own. But there is good research on the part that happens inside you, and that part is worth keeping.

What It Gets Right

Identity drives behavior. Psychologists who study "possible selves" have found that a clear picture of who you could become, paired with concrete strategies to get there, can change what people actually do. In one study, students who worked on their future selves and the steps to reach them did better in school than a comparison group.

You notice what you expect. Once you assume you're someone who gets hired, you read the job post differently. You apply. You follow up. The assumption changes what you see as possible on a regular Tuesday.

Where People Get Stuck

If you've tried the law of assumption and thought "this doesn't work," you're not crazy for wanting it to. You were probably doing the passive version. You assumed, felt good about it, then kept every habit exactly the same and waited for the universe to text back.

That's a known trap. Research by Gabriele Oettingen and colleagues found that vividly imagining an ideal future, with no thought about the path, can lower the energy you have to go after it. The good feeling stands in for the work. Keep the dream, and attach a next step to the assumption.

How to Use It This Week

  1. Write the assumption in the present tense. "I'm someone who's booked out as a photographer." One sentence, specific.
  2. Describe a normal day as that person. Not the big moment. A Wednesday. What do you do at 9am? What's on your calendar?
  3. Find one thing that person does that you don't. Posts their work every week? Has a rate sheet? Says no to free shoots?
  4. Do that one thing this week. Pick a day and a time. Small and slightly uncomfortable is right.
  5. Keep proof. Write down what happened. Proof is what makes the next assumption easier to believe.

Tip: add an "if-then" plan for the moment you'll want to skip it. "If I feel like the post isn't good enough, then I post it anyway and fix it next week." Research on plans like this shows they make follow-through more likely.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the law of assumption?

The law of assumption is the idea, taught by Neville Goddard, that your life reflects what you assume is true about yourself, so you change it by living from the assumption that you already have what you want.

What is the difference between the law of assumption and the law of attraction?

The law of attraction focuses on positive thinking drawing positive things to you. The law of assumption focuses on your self-concept and living as if your desire is already true.

Does the law of assumption work?

There's no evidence that assuming something makes it appear on its own. The useful part is identity: acting like the person who already has it can change your choices, especially when you pair it with a specific next step.

How do I start using the law of assumption?

Write your assumption in the present tense, describe a normal day as that person, pick one thing they do that you don't and do it this week at a set day and time.

Why isn't the law of assumption working for me?

Often it's the passive version: assuming and feeling good, then changing nothing. Research suggests imagining a perfect future without a plan can drain motivation, so attach a small weekly action to the assumption.
